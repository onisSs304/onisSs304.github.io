around some concentration inequalities and their applications

Introduction

In this post, we will study some popular and basic concentration inequalities. We will use this framework to provide some concentration guarantees of minimizers obtained via Empirical Risk Minimization. Roughly speaking, we try to understand the high-probability behaviour of the empirical minimizers around the true minimizer. Furthermore, I will also re-introduce the notion of algorithmic stability and deduce a simple convergence rate under stability conditions by using concentration inequalities.

Classical Inequalities

Markov's Inequality

We begin with the most elegant, yet powerful Markov inequality. Then we will see how this inequality can be applied in several modern inequalities.

Theorem — Markov's Inequality
Let \( X \) be a non-negative integrable random variable. Then for all \( t \geq 0 \): \[ \mathbb{P}(X \geq t) \leq \frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{t} \]
Proof

We have:

\[ \mathbb{E}[X] = \int_0^{+\infty} x\, d\mathbb{P}(x) = \int_0^t x\, d\mathbb{P}(x) + \int_t^{+\infty} x\, d\mathbb{P}(x) \geq 0 + t\int_t^{+\infty} d\mathbb{P}(x) = t\,\mathbb{P}(X \geq t) \]

This is equivalent to the inequality in the statement.

□

Some of us (including me) may feel more familiar with Chebyshev's inequality, but it is actually a direct consequence of Markov's inequality. Let \( Y \) be an integrable random variable. Replacing \( X \) by \( |Y - \mathbb{E}[Y]| \) in Markov's inequality: \[ \mathbb{P}(|Y - \mathbb{E}[Y]| \geq t) = \mathbb{P}((Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])^2 \geq t^2) \leq \frac{\mathbb{E}[(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])^2]}{t^2} = \frac{\mathrm{Var}(Y)}{t^2} \]

Now we come with Chernoff's trick (which will be used frequently for further inequalities) to get exponential bounds that work well for all types of random variables. Since \( e^x \) is positive and strictly increasing, for any \( \lambda > 0 \): \[ \mathbb{P}(X \geq t) = \mathbb{P}(e^{\lambda X} \geq e^{\lambda t}) \leq e^{-\lambda t}\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X}] \] The remaining task is to bound \( \mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X}] \) and then find the value of \( \lambda \) giving the optimal bound. Some examples:

Example 1. Let \( X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2) \). We have: \[ \mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X}] = e^{\lambda^2\sigma^2/2 + \lambda\mu} \implies \mathbb{P}(X \geq t) \leq e^{\lambda^2\sigma^2/2 + \lambda(\mu - t)} \] The optimal \( \lambda \) is \( \lambda = (t - \mu)/\sigma^2 \), giving: \[ \mathbb{P}(X \geq t) \leq \exp\!\left(-\frac{(t-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \]

Example 2. Let \( X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n \) be i.i.d. \( \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2) \) and \( S = X_1 + \cdots + X_n \). Applying Chernoff's trick and independence: \[ \mathbb{P}(S - \mathbb{E}[S] \geq t) \leq \exp(-\lambda t)\prod_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X_i - \mathbb{E}[\lambda X_i]}] \] Using the previous example: \[ \mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X_i - \mathbb{E}[\lambda X_i]}] = \exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda^2\sigma^2}{2}\right) \implies \mathbb{P}(S - \mathbb{E}[S] \geq t) \leq \exp\!\left(-\lambda t + \frac{n\lambda^2\sigma^2}{2}\right) \] Optimizing at \( \lambda = t/(n\sigma^2) \): \[ \mathbb{P}(S - \mathbb{E}[S] \geq t) \leq \exp\!\left(-\frac{t^2}{2n\sigma^2}\right) \]

Hoeffding's Inequality

In Chernoff's trick, if we do not have access to the distribution of \( X \), we cannot compute \( \mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X}] \) explicitly. Therefore, finding a bound for this term is a reasonable option. We begin with Hoeffding's lemma, which is the key to the final inequality.

Lemma — Hoeffding's Lemma
Let \( X \) be a real-valued random variable with zero expectation (\( \mathbb{E}[X] = 0 \)) and \( a \leq X \leq b \) with probability 1. Then for all \( \lambda > 0 \): \[ \mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X}] \leq \exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda^2(b-a)^2}{8}\right) \]
Proof

Write \( X = (1-t)a + tb \) where \( t \in [0,1] \) and \( t = (X-a)/(b-a) \). Since \( e^x \) is convex:

\[ \exp(\lambda X) = \exp((1-t)\lambda a + t\lambda b) \leq (1-t)\exp(\lambda a) + t\exp(\lambda b) \]

Taking expectations:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda X)] \leq \exp(\lambda a)\mathbb{E}[1-t] + \exp(\lambda b)\mathbb{E}[t] = \exp(\lambda a)\frac{b}{b-a} + \exp(\lambda b)\frac{-a}{b-a} \]

Let \( \gamma = -a/(b-a) \) and \( u = (b-a)\lambda \). Then:

\[ \log(\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda X)]) \leq \lambda a + \log(1 - \gamma + \gamma e^u) =: \phi(u) \]

By Taylor's theorem, there exists \( x \in [0, u] \) such that \( \phi(u) = \phi(0) + u\phi'(0) + \frac{u^2}{2}\phi''(x) \). We compute \( \phi(0) = 0 \), \( \phi'(0) = 0 \), and:

\[ \phi''(x) = \frac{\gamma(1-\gamma)e^u}{(1-\gamma+\gamma e^u)^2} \leq \frac{1}{4} \]

where the final inequality comes from the AM-GM inequality. Therefore:

\[ \log(\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda X)]) \leq \frac{\lambda^2(b-a)^2}{8} \]
□
Theorem — Hoeffding's Inequality
Let \( X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n \) be \( n \) independent random variables with \( X_i \in [a_i, b_i] \), and \( S_n = X_1 + \cdots + X_n \). Then: \[ \mathbb{P}(S_n - \mathbb{E}[S_n] \geq t) \leq \exp\!\left(\frac{-2t^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n(b_i - a_i)^2}\right) \]
Proof

Using Chernoff's trick and Hoeffding's lemma:

\begin{align*} \mathbb{P}(S_n - \mathbb{E}[S_n] \geq t) &\leq \exp\!\left(-\lambda t + \sum_{i=1}^n \log(\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda(X_i - \mathbb{E}[X_i])}])\right) \\ &\leq \exp\!\left(-\lambda t + \lambda^2\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{(b_i - a_i)^2}{8}\right) \end{align*}

The minimum of the right-hand side is at \( \lambda = 4t / \sum_{i=1}^n(b_i - a_i)^2 \), giving:

\[ \mathbb{P}(S_n - \mathbb{E}[S_n] \geq t) \leq \exp\!\left(-\frac{2t^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n(b_i - a_i)^2}\right) \]
□

However, Hoeffding's inequality ignores second-order information such as variance. In the next subsections, we will consider inequalities that bring second-order information into play. The bound may become tighter or not depending on the variance of the random variable considered.

Bennett's Inequality

Theorem — Bennett's Inequality
Let \( X_1, \ldots, X_n \) be independent real-valued random variables with zero mean and \( |X_i| \leq 1 \) with probability 1. Then for any \( t > 0 \): \[ \mathbb{P}(S_n \geq t) \leq \exp\!\left(-v\, h\!\left(\frac{t}{v}\right)\right) \] where \( S_n = X_1 + \cdots + X_n \), \( v = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}[X_i^2] \), and \( h(u) = (1+u)\log(1+u) - u \).
Proof

Step 1. We prove that \( \log(\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda S}]) \leq v(e^\lambda - \lambda - 1) \).

Since \( u \mapsto (e^u - u - 1)/u^2 \) is increasing and \( X_1 \leq 1 \): \[ e^{\lambda X_1} \leq \lambda X_1 + 1 + X_1^2(e^\lambda - \lambda - 1) \] Taking expectations: \[ \mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X_1}] \leq 1 + \mathbb{E}[X_1^2](e^\lambda - \lambda - 1) \] Therefore, using concavity of \( \log(1+u) \) and \( \log(1+u) \leq u \): \[ \log(\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda S}]) \leq \sum_{i=1}^n \log(1 + \mathbb{E}[X_i^2](e^\lambda - \lambda - 1)) \leq n\log\!\left(1 + \frac{v}{n}(e^\lambda - \lambda - 1)\right) \leq v(e^\lambda - \lambda - 1) \]

Step 2. Using Chernoff's trick:

\[ \mathbb{P}(S_n \geq t) \leq e^{-\lambda t} \mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda S_n}] \leq \exp(-\lambda t + v(e^\lambda - \lambda - 1)) \]

The right-hand side is minimized at \( \lambda = \log(1 + t/v) \), giving: \[ \mathbb{P}(S_n \geq t) \leq \exp\!\left(-vh\!\left(\frac{t}{v}\right)\right) \]

□

Bernstein's Inequality

Bernstein's inequality is obtained by further bounding the function \( h \) in Bennett's inequality. Indeed: \[ h(u) = (1+u)\log(1+u) - u \geq \frac{3u^2}{2(u+3)} \] The proof is simple: consider the derivative of their difference and show it is always non-negative. Therefore, Bernstein's inequality is: \[ \mathbb{P}(S_n \geq t) \leq \exp\!\left(-vh\!\left(\frac{t}{v}\right)\right) \leq \exp\!\left(\frac{-3t^2}{2t + 6v}\right) \] Bernstein's inequality is essentially a user-friendly approximation of Bennett's inequality. In the applications section, we will see how Bernstein-type inequalities improve the rate of convergence of empirical minimizers.

The above result works under the same conditions as Bennett's inequality. Alternatively, it still holds under a weaker condition than the boundedness assumption: \[ \forall\, k \geq 3: \mathbb{E}[(X_+)^k] \leq \frac{1}{2} k!\, \mathbb{E}[X^2] b^{k-2} \] where \( X_+ = \max(X, 0) \) and \( b \) is a parameter. In this case: \[ \mathbb{P}(S_n \geq t) \leq \exp\!\left(\frac{-3t^2}{2bt + 6v}\right) \]

McDiarmid's Inequality

Theorem — McDiarmid's Inequality
Let \( X_1, \ldots, X_n \) be independent random variables taking values in \( \mathcal{X} \). Let \( f : \mathcal{X}^n \to \mathbb{R} \) satisfy, for all \( i \) and all \( x_1, \ldots, x_n, x_i' \in \mathcal{X} \): \[ |f(x_1, \ldots, x_{i-1}, x_i, \ldots, x_n) - f(x_1, \ldots, x_{i-1}, x_i', \ldots, x_n)| \leq c_i \] Then for all \( \epsilon > 0 \): \[ \mathbb{P}(f - \mathbb{E}[f] \geq \epsilon) \leq \exp\!\left(\frac{-\epsilon^2}{2\sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2}\right) \]
Proof

The proof is based on Hoeffding's lemma. Define:

\[ V_i = \mathbb{E}[f \mid X_1, \ldots, X_i] - \mathbb{E}[f \mid X_1, \ldots, X_{i-1}] \implies f - \mathbb{E}[f] = \sum_{i=1}^n V_i \]

We cannot use independence directly to decompose the Chernoff bound. Instead, we apply Azuma's inequality (the martingale version of Hoeffding's inequality):

Lemma — Azuma's Inequality
Let \( Z_0, \ldots, Z_n \) be a martingale with respect to \( \mathcal{F}_0 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq \mathcal{F}_n \) such that \( |Y_i| = |Z_i - Z_{i-1}| \leq c_i \) for all \( i \). Then: \[ \mathbb{P}[Z_n - Z_0 \geq t] \leq \exp\!\left(\frac{-t^2}{2\sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2}\right) \]
Proof of Azuma's inequality

For any \( \lambda > 0 \), by Chernoff's bound:

\[ \mathbb{P}[Z_n - Z_0 \geq t] \leq e^{-\lambda t}\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda(Z_n - Z_0)}] \]

Conditioning on \( \mathcal{F}_{n-1} \):

\begin{align*} \mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda(Z_n - Z_0)}] &= \mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda(Z_{n-1} - Z_0)}\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda Y_n} \mid \mathcal{F}_{n-1}]] \end{align*}

Using convexity of \( e^x \) with \( \alpha = Y_n/c_n \) and \( M = \lambda c_n \): \[ e^{\lambda Y_n} \leq \left(\frac{1 + Y_n/c_n}{2}\right)e^{\lambda c_n} + \left(\frac{1 - Y_n/c_n}{2}\right)e^{-\lambda c_n} \] Using \( \mathbb{E}[Y_n \mid \mathcal{F}_{n-1}] = 0 \) and \( (e^x + e^{-x})/2 \leq e^{x^2/2} \): \[ \mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda Y_n} \mid \mathcal{F}_{n-1}] \leq \frac{e^{\lambda c_n} + e^{-\lambda c_n}}{2} \leq e^{(\lambda c_n)^2/2} \]

Iterating over all steps:

\[ \mathbb{P}[Z_n - Z_0 \geq t] \leq \exp\!\left(-\lambda t + \frac{\lambda^2}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2\right) \]

Optimizing over \( \lambda = t/\sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2 \) gives the result.

□

Let \( Z_i = \mathbb{E}[f \mid X_1, \ldots, X_i] \). By the tower property of conditional expectation, \( \mathbb{E}[Z_{i+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_i] = Z_i \), so \( (Z_i)_i \) is a martingale. With \( V_i = Z_i - Z_{i-1} \), it remains to show the boundedness condition. Indeed:

\begin{align*} |V_i| &\leq \sup_{x \in \mathcal{X}} |\mathbb{E}[f(X_1,\ldots,x,\ldots,X_n) \mid X_1,\ldots,X_{i-1},X_i = x] - \mathbb{E}[f \mid X_1,\ldots,X_{i-1}]| \\ &= \sup_{x \in \mathcal{X}} |\mathbb{E}[f(X_1,\ldots,x,\ldots,X_n) - f(X_1,\ldots,X_n) \mid X_1,\ldots,X_{i-1}]| \leq c_i \end{align*}

Applying Azuma's inequality gives the result.

□
Applications

In this section, we focus on applications of these inequalities in providing concentration guarantees, especially in the field of Statistical Learning Theory.

Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM)

Empirical risk minimization is a central tool in modern statistics and machine learning. We choose the function \( f_n \) that minimizes the empirical risk over a class \( \mathcal{F} \): \[ f_n \in \arg\min_{f \in \mathcal{F}} R_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n l(Z_i, f) \] where \( Z_1, \ldots, Z_n \) are i.i.d. from an unknown distribution \( P \) and \( l \) is the loss function. For example, in least mean square problems with \( Z = (X,Y) \): \( l(Z, f) = \|Y - f(X)\|^2 \).

The corresponding population risk is \( R(f) = \mathbb{E}[l(Z,f)] \), and \( f^\star = \arg\min_{f \in \mathcal{F}} R(f) \). A key task in statistical learning is to find guarantees on the excess risk \( R(f_n) - R(f^\star) \).

Regression. \( Z = (X,Y) \) and \( l(Z, f) = (Y - f(X))^2 \). The Bayes predictor is \( f^\star(X) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X] \), but since the distribution of \( (X,Y) \) is unknown, we restrict to a smaller class (e.g. linear functions) and use ERM on available data. By the law of large numbers, the ERM minimizer converges almost surely to the true minimizer.

Binary classification. \( Z = (X,Y) \) with \( Y \in \{0,1\} \). We use the cross-entropy loss: \[ l(Z, f) = -Y\log f(X) - (1-Y)\log(1-f(X)) \] The Bayes predictor is \( f^\star(x) = \mathbb{P}(Y=1 \mid X=x) \). A popular parametrization uses the logistic function \( p_f(x) = 1/(1 + \exp(-f(x))) \), which gives the logistic regression loss: \[ l(Z, f) = \log(1 + \exp(-f(X))) - Yf(X) \]

Our goal. Understand how quickly \( f_n \) approaches \( f^\star \) as \( n \) grows. We will derive simple convergence rates via concentration inequalities, focusing on the excess risk \( R(f_n) - R(f^\star) \).

Generalization Error

To bound the excess risk, we first transform it into a more convenient form. Indeed: \begin{align*} R(f_n) - R(f^\star) &= R(f_n) - R_n(f_n) + R_n(f_n) - R_n(f^\star) + R_n(f^\star) - R(f^\star) \\ &\leq \sup_{f \in \mathcal{F}} |R(f) - R_n(f)| + 0 + \sup_{f \in \mathcal{F}} |R(f) - R_n(f)| \\ &\leq 2\sup_{f \in \mathcal{F}} |R(f) - R_n(f)| \end{align*} Since \( R(f) = \mathbb{E}[R_n(f)] \), we can in principle apply the concentration inequalities from the previous section. However, the difficulty comes from the supremum, which prevents direct application.

We overcome this by a union-of-events argument:

\begin{align*} &\left\{\sup_{f \in \mathcal{F}} |R(f) - R_n(f)| \geq t\right\} = \bigcup_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \{|R(f) - R_n(f)| \geq t\} \\ &\implies \mathbb{P}\!\left(\sup_{f \in \mathcal{F}} |R(f) - R_n(f)| \geq t\right) \leq \sum_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \mathbb{P}(|R(f) - R_n(f)| \geq t) \end{align*}

The bound depends on the complexity of \( \mathcal{F} \). Suppose \( \mathcal{F} \) is finite with \( |\mathcal{F}| = N \) and \( l(Z_i, f) \in [a, b] \) for all \( i \). Using the two-sided Hoeffding's inequality: \[ \mathbb{P}(|R_n(f) - R(f)| \geq t) \leq 2\exp\!\left(\frac{-2nt^2}{(b-a)^2}\right) \] Denoting the right-hand side by \( \delta \), with probability at least \( 1-\delta \): \[ |R_n(f) - R(f)| \leq (b-a)\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2n}} \] Using the union bound over \( N \) functions: \[ \mathbb{P}\!\left(\sup_{f \in \mathcal{F}} |R(f) - R_n(f)| \geq t\right) \leq 2N\exp\!\left(\frac{-2nt^2}{(b-a)^2}\right) \] Therefore, with probability at least \( 1-\delta \): \[ R(f_n) - R(f^\star) \leq (b-a)\sqrt{\frac{\log(2N/\delta)}{2n}} \]

Limitations. Hoeffding's inequality uses only the boundedness property to produce a bound with convergence rate \( 1/\sqrt{n} \), but ignores variance information. Bernstein-type inequalities can overcome this. Furthermore, for infinite-dimensional classes \( \mathcal{F} \), the situation is more complicated — this will be addressed in a future post.

Fast Rate

In this subsection, we only consider bounded losses in \( [-1, 1] \), i.e. \( |l(Z, f) - \mathbb{E}[l(Z,f)]| \leq 1 \) for all \( f \in \mathcal{F} \). This satisfies all conditions for Bernstein's inequality: \[ \mathbb{P}(|R_n(f) - \mathbb{E}[R_n(f)]| \geq t) \leq 2\exp\!\left(\frac{-3nt^2}{2t + 6\sigma^2}\right) \] where \( v = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{Var}(l(Z_i,f)) \) and \( \sigma^2 = v/n \). With probability at least \( 1-\delta \): \[ |R_n(f) - \mathbb{E}[R_n(f)]| \leq \frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n} + \sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)^2}{9n^2} + \frac{2\log(2/\delta)\sigma^2}{n}} \] Using the inequality \( a + \sqrt{a^2 + b} \leq 2a + \sqrt{b} \) for \( a, b \geq 0 \): \[ |R_n(f) - \mathbb{E}[R_n(f)]| \leq \frac{2\log(2/\delta)}{n} + \sqrt{\frac{2\log(2/\delta)\sigma^2}{n}} \] If \( \sigma^2 \approx 0 \) as \( n \) grows, we gain a convergence rate up to \( 1/n \). Understanding the noise of the losses is therefore very important in learning problems.

Noise Condition: Bernstein's Condition

In the last subsection, we discussed how bounded losses may produce faster convergence rates. However, this condition is rather abstract. We now consider a more systematic regime: the Variance-Expectation (or Bernstein's) condition.

Bernstein's condition. There exist \( \beta > 0 \) and \( \alpha \in [0,1] \) such that for all \( f \in \mathcal{F} \): \[ \mathrm{Var}(l(Z,f) - l(Z,f^\star)) \leq \beta\,\mathbb{E}[l(Z,f) - l(Z,f^\star)]^\alpha \] As \( f_n \) approaches \( f^\star \), the right-hand side converges to zero, controlling the variance of \( l(Z,f_n) \):

  • If \( \alpha = 0 \), we only gain the slow rate as in Hoeffding's inequality.
  • If \( 0 < \alpha \leq 1 \), we get a better rate, specified in the theorem below.
Theorem — Fast Rate under Bernstein's Condition
Suppose \( \mathcal{F} = \{f_1, \ldots, f_N\} \) is a finite class with bounded losses \( |l(Z,f) - l(Z,f^\star)| \leq 1 \) and satisfying Bernstein's condition with parameters \( (\beta, \alpha) \). Let \( f^\star = \arg\min_{f \in \mathcal{F}} R(f) \) and \( f_n \) be the ERM minimizer. Then with probability at least \( 1-\delta \): \[ R(f_n) - R(f^\star) \leq \frac{4\log(N/\delta)}{3n} + \left(\frac{2\beta\log(N/\delta)}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}} \]
Proof

Let \( X(f) = l(Z,f) - l(Z,f^\star) \), \( X_i(f) = l(Z_i,f) - l(Z_i,f^\star) \), and \( \Delta l(f) = R(f) - R(f^\star) \). Define: \[ R_X(f) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i(f) = R_n(f) - R_n(f^\star) \] By Bernstein's inequality, with probability at least \( 1-\delta \): \[ \Delta l(f) - R_X(f) \leq \frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{3n} + \sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/\delta)\,\mathrm{Var}(X(f))}{n}} \leq \frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{3n} + \sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/\delta)\beta(\Delta l(f))^\alpha}{n}} \] Using the union bound over \( |\mathcal{F}| = N \) functions, with probability at least \( 1-\delta \): \[ \sup_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \Delta l(f) - R_X(f) \leq \frac{2\log(N/\delta)}{3n} + \sqrt{\frac{2\log(N/\delta)\beta(\Delta l(f))^\alpha}{n}} \] Injecting \( f_n \) and using \( R_X(f_n) \leq 0 \): \[ \Delta l(f_n) \leq \frac{2\log(N/\delta)}{3n} + \sqrt{\frac{2\log(N/\delta)\beta(\Delta l(f_n))^\alpha}{n}} \] Let \( x = \Delta l(f_n) \), \( a = \frac{2\log(N/\delta)}{3n} \), \( b = \frac{2\log(N/\delta)\beta}{n} \). We have \( x \leq a + \sqrt{bx^\alpha} \). Applying Young's inequality with \( u = x^{\alpha/2} \), \( v = \sqrt{b} \), \( p = 2/\alpha \), \( q = 2/(2-\alpha) \): \[ \sqrt{bx^\alpha} \leq \frac{\alpha x}{2} + b^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}\frac{2-\alpha}{2} \] Using \( \sqrt{bx^\alpha} \geq x - a \): \[ x - a \leq \frac{\alpha x}{2} + b^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}\frac{2-\alpha}{2} \implies x \leq \frac{2a}{2-\alpha} + b^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}} \leq 2a + b^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}} \] Therefore, with probability at least \( 1-\delta \): \[ R(f_n) - R(f^\star) \leq \frac{4\log(N/\delta)}{3n} + \left(\frac{2\log(N/\delta)\beta}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}} \] As \( n \) grows: \[ R(f_n) - R(f^\star) \leq O\!\left(\left(\frac{\beta\log(N/\delta)}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}}\right) \]

□

Algorithmic Stability

Firstly, we introduce the definition of algorithmic stability from [Bousquet & Elisseeff, 2002]. For convenience, we reuse the same notation throughout this section. Let \(S=\{z_1=(x_1,y_1),\ldots,z_n=(x_n,y_n)\}\) be a collection of \(n\) independent sample points, where \(\mathcal{X}\) and \(\mathcal{Y}\) denote the input and output spaces, respectively.

A learning algorithm \(A\) is a mapping from \((\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^n\) to a learned predictor, denoted by \(A_S\), which depends on the training set \(S\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^n\). Following the notation of the original paper, let \(z_i'\) be an independent copy of \(z_i\), and define the modified training set \[ S_i=\{z_1,\ldots,z_i',\ldots,z_n\}. \]

To evaluate the performance of the learning algorithm, we consider a loss function \[ l(Z,f)=c(f(X),Y), \] where \(c:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) is the cost function. In our setting, the learned predictor is \(A_S\), so the loss is written as \[ l(A_S,Z)=c(A_S(X),Y). \]

The empirical error over the training set \(S\) is defined as \[ R_n(S)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}l(A_S,z_i), \] while the (expected) risk is \[ R(S)=\mathbb{E}_z[l(A_S,z)], \] where \(z\) is an independent sample having the same distribution as the training examples.

Note that \(R(S)\) is still a random variable since it depends on the random training sample \(S\). Consequently, we may take the expectation with respect to \(S\). Moreover, the expected empirical error satisfies \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}_S[R_n(S)] &=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_S[l(A_S,z_i)] \\ &=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{S,z_i'}[l(A_S,z_i)] \\ &=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{S,z_i'}[l(A_{S_i},z_i')] \\ &=\mathbb{E}_{S,z_i'}[l(A_{S_i},z_i')]. \end{aligned} \]

Definition — Uniform Stability

A learning algorithm \(A\) has uniform stability \(\beta\) with respect to the loss function \(l\) if

\[ \forall\,S,\; \forall\,z_i,z\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}, \qquad |l(A_S,z)-l(A_{S_i},z)| \le \beta. \]

Here, \(A_S\) denotes the predictor learned from the original training set \(S\), whereas \(A_{S_i}\) is the predictor obtained after replacing the \(i\)-th training example by its independent copy \(z_i'\).

Throughout the remainder of this section, we also assume that the loss function is bounded. Specifically,

\[ 0\le l(A_S,z)\le M, \qquad \forall\,S,\;\forall\,z. \]

Under this boundedness assumption together with the uniform stability condition, our main objective is to study the high-probability behaviour of the generalization error \[ R(S)-R_n(S). \]

Theorem — Stability Generalization Bound
Let \( A \) be a \( \beta \)-uniformly stable algorithm and suppose \( 0 \leq l(A_S, z) \leq M \) for all \( S, z \). Then for any \( n \geq 1 \) and \( \delta \in (0,1) \), with probability at least \( 1-\delta \): \[ R_n(f) - R(f) \leq \beta + (n\beta + M)\sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{n}} \]
Proof

Denote \( X(S) = R_n(S) - R(S) \). We will verify some additional assumptions to be able to use McDiarmid's inequality. We start by checking the boundedness condition:

\begin{align*} |X(S) - X(S_i)| &\leq |R_n(S) - R_n(S_i)| + |R(S) - R(S_i)| \\ &\leq \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j \neq i} |l(A_S, z_j) - l(A_{S_i}, z_j)| + \frac{1}{n}|l(A_S, z_i) - l(A_{S_i}, z_i')| + |\mathbb{E}[l(A_S, z) - l(A_{S_i}, z)]| \\ &\leq \frac{(n-1)\beta}{n} + \frac{1}{n}|l(A_S, z_i) - l(A_{S_i}, z_i)| + |l(A_{S_i}, z_i)| + |l(A_{S_i}, z_i')| + \beta \\ &\leq 2\beta + \frac{2M}{n} \end{align*}

Therefore, \( c_i = 2\beta + 2M/n \) in McDiarmid's inequality, so:

\[ \mathbb{P}_S\!\left(X(S) - \mathbb{E}_S[X(S)] \geq t\right) \leq \exp\!\left(-\frac{nt^2}{2(n\beta + M)^2}\right) \]

It remains to calculate \( \mathbb{E}_S[X(S)] \), which can be bounded tightly by the uniform stability property:

\begin{align*} \mathbb{E}_S[X(S)] &= \mathbb{E}_S[R_n(S)] - \mathbb{E}_{S,z}[l(A_S, z)] \\ &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}_S[l(A_S, z_i)] - \mathbb{E}_{S,z}[l(A_S, z)] \\ &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n \mathbb{E}_{S, z_i'}[l(A_S, z_j)] - \mathbb{E}_{S,z}[l(A_S, z)] \\ &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n \mathbb{E}_{S, z_i'}[l(A_{S_i}, z_i')] - \mathbb{E}_{S,z}[l(A_S, z)] \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{S, z_i'}[l(A_{S_i}, z_i')] - \mathbb{E}_{S,z}[l(A_S, z)] \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{S, z_i'}[l(A_{S_i}, z_i') - l(A_S, z_i')] \geq -\beta \end{align*}

Then injecting into the above inequality:

\[ \mathbb{P}_S(X(S) \leq \beta + t) \geq 1 - \exp\!\left(-\frac{nt^2}{2(n\beta + M)^2}\right) \]

Assigning \( \delta = \exp\!\left(-\dfrac{nt^2}{2(n\beta+M)^2}\right) \), then \( t = (n\beta + M)\sqrt{\dfrac{2\log(1/\delta)}{n}} \). Finally, with probability at least \( 1 - \delta \):

\[ R_n(f) - R(f) \leq \beta + (n\beta + M)\sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{n}} \]
□

This theorem first appeared in [Bousquet & Elisseeff, 2002], which provided the first definition of uniform stability and convergence guarantees for stable learning algorithms. However, it suffers from a major limitation regarding the boundedness of the loss function, which may narrow the range of applicability.

Conclusion

Together, we have seen some applications of concentration inequalities in several settings such as Statistical Learning Theory and Algorithmic Stability Analysis. However, most of the above results assume the ERM minimizers are found over a finite class, which narrows a wide range of problems. To handle the infinite case, we would need terms that measure the class complexity rather than simply counting the number of functions.
