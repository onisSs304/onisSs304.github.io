some ideas on analyzing momentum-based methods via large deviation theory (part 2)

Reduced-ODE to Approximate Path of SGDM
A General Framework

Before diving into the LDP, we introduce the iterates of SGDM: \[ \begin{cases} x_{n+1} = x_n + \eta v_{n+1} \\ v_{n+1} = \beta v_n - \nabla G(x_n, \omega_n) = \beta v_n - \nabla f(x_n) + U(x_n, \omega_n) \\ x_0, v_0 \in \mathbb{R}^d \end{cases} \tag{1} \] and the continuous-time form of SGDM is defined in part 1: \[ \begin{cases} \dot{X}_t = W_t \\[6pt] \dot{W}_t = \dfrac{1}{\eta}\left(-(1-\beta)W_t - G(X_t, \omega_{\lfloor t/\eta \rfloor})\right) \end{cases} \tag{2} \]

In the work of [Azizian et al., 2024], they rely on the foundational work of [Freidlin & Wentzell, 1998] to derive the LDP for iterates of SGD. However, their theory cannot fully describe the situation where the fast motion (the noise in SGD) depends on the slow motion and the previous states — roughly speaking, when the slow variable is frozen, the fast variable becomes a Markov chain on an infinite state space. In the previous post, we overcame this issue by refining the proof, but I believe that it does not represent perfectly the dynamics or the benefits brought by momentum. In this post, we focus on the theory of large deviations for a more general system of discrete-time processes with averaging. For the base works, I refer to [Kifer, 2008] and [Gulinsky & Veretennikov, 1993]. My objective is to deduce the LDP for the following general system of discrete-time processes with averaging: \[ \begin{cases} x_{n+1} = x_n + \eta\, g(x_n, v_{n+1}) \\ v_{n+1} = v_n + a(x_n, v_n) + b(x_n, v_n)W_n =: P(v_n, x_n, W_n) \end{cases} \tag{3} \] This is not the standard form in the main results of either [Gulinsky & Veretennikov, 1993] or [Kifer, 2008], but we will refine the analysis to obtain the LDP for this system. One more special feature of system (3): it covers the iterates of vanilla SGD, SGDM, or even NAG. Focusing on SGDM, we choose \( g(x,y) = y \), \( a(x,y) = -(1-\beta)y - \nabla f(x) \), \( b(x,y) = \mathrm{Cov}(U(x,\omega)) \); and \( \beta = 0 \) adapts to vanilla SGD. So if we find a method for this system, this new framework will be more general than [Azizian et al., 2024] and represents a promising direction.

Averaging Principle

Define the frozen Markov chain \( (v_n^x)_n \) by: \[ v_{n+1}^x = \beta v_n^x - \nabla f(x) + U(x, \omega_n) \] It is obviously a Markov chain with infinite state space. We assume that \( (v_n^x)_n \) has an invariant measure \( \mu \). In a general setting, we can proceed with results about averaging methods without figuring out the invariant measure explicitly. But in our problem, it is better if we know the exact formula, as it will serve further analysis. Let \( (\mathbb{R}^d, \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^d)) \) be the measurable space we work on. Based on the chain defined above, we introduce the transition probability kernel: \[ K^x(a, A) = \mathbb{P}(U(x, \omega_n) \in \{y - \beta a + \nabla f(x) \mid y \in A\}) \] so the invariant measure is given by: \[ \mu^x(A) = \int K(a, A)\,\mu^x(da), \quad \forall\, A \in \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^d) \] And define the function: \[ h(x) = \int g(x,v)\,\mu^x(dv) = \int v\,\mu^x(dv) = \mathbb{E}^x[V] \] if \( V \) follows the invariant measure. However, we can compute this function another way: since the above Markov chain is defined linearly, we simply take expectations on both sides. If \( v_0^x \sim \mu^x \) then so does \( v_{n+1}^x \). Then: \[ \mathbb{E}[v_{n+1}^x] = \mathbb{E}[v_n^x] - \nabla f(x) \iff \mathbb{E}[V] = \mathbb{E}[v_{n+1}^x] = \mathbb{E}[v_n^x] = -\frac{1}{1-\beta}\nabla f(x) \] Therefore \( h(x) = -\frac{1}{1-\beta}\nabla f(x) \) and the averaged system is \( \dot{\bar{x}}(t) = h(\bar{x}(t)) \). We then define the linearly interpolated process: \[ X(t) = (t-n)x_{n+1} + (n+1-t)x_n, \quad \forall\, t \in [n, n+1], \quad x(t) = X(t/\eta) \] Then for \( T > 0 \), under necessary assumptions: \[ \lim_{\eta \to 0} \mathbb{E}\!\left[\sup_{0 \leq t \leq T} |x(t) - \bar{x}(t)|\right] = 0 \] In Chap. 6 of [Gulinsky & Veretennikov, 1993], a high-level set of assumptions for the above result is listed. Specifically, we need \( v_n \) to belong to a compact set, which happens when the noise also obeys this property. If we can prove that \( x_n \) belongs to a compact set, then a growth condition on the noise is sufficient. This is similar in spirit to the set of assumptions in [Azizian et al., 2024].

Action Functional

Now suppose that \( v_n \) belongs to a compact set, meaning it is bounded by some constant \( K \). Obviously \( g(x,y) = y \) is 1-Lipschitz. Recall that \( U(x,\omega) \) is uniformly non-degenerate, i.e. its covariance is positive definite and satisfies a quadratic growth condition. We define the Hamiltonian:

Proposition
Let \( (v_n)_n \) be defined as above. Under the above conditions, the following limit exists uniformly in \( x, x' \) over a compact set: \[ H(x, x', \alpha) = \lim_{k \to +\infty} \frac{1}{k} \log\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left\langle\alpha, \sum_{j=1}^k g(x', v_j^x)\right\rangle\right] = \lim_{k \to +\infty} \frac{1}{k} \log\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left\langle\alpha, \sum_{j=1}^k v_j^x\right\rangle\right] \]

The proof of this proposition is based on Proposition 2.2.11 of [Kifer, 2008]. Note that there is a small difference in the statement, but this is not a major issue: we can bound the expectation in the limit by a standard expression and then use the available result in Proposition 2.2.11 of Kifer's work to demonstrate the existence of the limit.

Connection to [Azizian et al., 2024]. In the absence of the momentum effect (\( \beta = 0 \)), \( v_{n+1}^x = -\nabla f(x) + U(x, \omega_n) \) is independent of all previous states, so: \[ H(x, \alpha) = \lim_{k \to +\infty} \frac{1}{k} \log\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left\langle\alpha, \sum_{j=1}^k v_j^x\right\rangle\right] = \log\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left\langle\alpha, -\nabla f(x) + U(x,\omega)\right\rangle\right] \] which recovers the Hamiltonians in their work. However, in our case, this seems impossible to compute exactly. But the existence is sufficient for further analysis.

The Lagrangian is given as the convex conjugate of the Hamiltonian: \[ L(x, \gamma) = \sup_\alpha\left(\langle\alpha, \gamma\rangle - H(x, x, \alpha)\right) \] Finally, the action functional is as usual and similar to [Azizian et al., 2024]: \[ S_{0T}(\phi) = \int_0^T L(\phi_t, \dot{\phi}_t)\, dt \] This functional is zero if \( \phi_t \) is a solution of the averaged system listed in the averaging principle section, and different from 0 for any other path. Note that this functional is taken from the continuous-time process, which may cause some issues for the discrete analysis. We could define a "discretized" version of the action functional as mentioned in the previous post (in the book Random Perturbations of Dynamical Systems by Y. Kifer). However, we hope that when \( \eta \to 0 \), we can use \( S_{0T} \) directly to obtain the LDP.

Large Deviation Principles

Recall that the process we work on is the continuous interpolated process \( x_t \) given by: \[ x_t = \left(\frac{t}{\eta} - n\right)(x_{n+1} - x_n) + x_n, \quad \forall\, t \in [n\eta, (n+1)\eta] \]

Definition — Laplace Principle
Let \( \{X^n, n \in \mathbb{N}\} \) be a sequence of random variables taking values in a Polish space \( \mathcal{X} \) and let \( I \) be a rate function on \( \mathcal{X} \). We say that \( \{X^n, n \in \mathbb{N}\} \) satisfies a Laplace principle with rate function \( I \) if for every bounded continuous function \( h : \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R} \): \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp(-nh(X^n))\right] = -\inf_{x \in \mathcal{X}}\{h(x) + I(x)\} \]

In some settings, the Laplace principle is equivalent to the LDP. So an interesting approach is to first prove the Laplace principle for \( x_t \) then transfer to the LDP. An interesting reference here is [Boué et al., 2000].

Another research direction to prove the LDP for the interpolated process \( x_t \) is to refine the analysis in [Kifer, 2008] and [Gulinsky & Veretennikov, 1993], since our recursion is not in the standard form of their analysis. However, their proof of the LDP is built upon the structure of the Lagrangians and Hamiltonians, so if we can demonstrate their existence and main functional properties, the refinement should not be too hard (I believe).

Conclusion

Until now, we have studied 2 ways to analyze stochastic momentum-based methods:

  • Building directly upon the framework of [Azizian et al., 2024], we could prove an informal result (if all necessary assumptions are verified): the invariant measure of SGDM around stable minima is larger than that of vanilla SGD. This explains an empirical observation: SGDM has a better ability in finding stable minima.
  • Providing a more general framework that could recover several types of SGD as in (3): Heavy-ball (momentum), Nesterov gradient descent, vanilla SGD, etc.

Challenges. A more general system would require more assumptions: compactness for \( v_n \), the noise, etc. Another difficulty is to adjust the proof of the LDP, which is quite challenging, and to prove the existence and functional properties of the Hamiltonians (convexity, regularity, ...) — properties that are not directly available due to a small change in the standard system. Besides that, analyzing attractors and transitions between attractors is also interesting, but I have not touched this sub-field yet. Although there are some efforts to work with attractors for (2) in continuous time, there are not many such works for discrete-time processes.
