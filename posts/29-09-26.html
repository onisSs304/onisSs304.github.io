Riemannian quotient structure of hypersphere kernels

Introduction

In Gaussian process regression with categorical inputs with a finite number of levels, we need to define a covariance matrix — typically symmetric positive definite matrices formed by the covariance function. Given a set of observations, parametrizing such a matrix is normally hard, especially when the number of levels becomes extremely large. There are many strategies to address this question, such as considering categorical variables as some unobserved continuous ones (latent variables) or fixing a constant covariance for all pairs of levels so we only need 2 parameters. Besides, the hypersphere parametrization has the advantage of being very rich, but has a very large number of parameters to optimize, which can be prohibitive when the number of levels increases. Another way to handle the large number of levels is proposed in [Roustant et al., 2020], where they use small matrix blocks in the whole kernel.

Given a set of observations, estimating the hypersphere matrices using usual optimization tools is extremely expensive when the number of parameters grows quadratically with respect to the number of levels. In this post, we will consider some common parametrizations and place them as a "point" in a certain Riemannian manifold. Once this is done, optimization tools on smooth manifolds would be really beneficial.

Gaussian Process with Categorical Inputs

Let us consider the case where we observe \( n \) continuous variables and \( m \) categorical variables with respectively \( C_1, \ldots, C_m \) levels. For the \( i \)-th categorical variable, the set of levels is \( Z_i = [1, \ldots, C_i] \), and the input space is: \[ W = \mathbb{R}^n \times Z_1 \times Z_2 \times \cdots \times Z_m \] Given a set of \( N \) observations \( ((w_1, y_1), \ldots, (w_N, y_N)) \in W^N \), we consider a Gaussian process regression defined on \( W \). Furthermore, we assume that \( y_i = \mu(w_i) + Z(w_i) + \epsilon_i \) where \( \epsilon_i \) are i.i.d. Gaussian noise, \( \mu \) is the trend part, and \( Z \) is the GP part. Our main objective is to characterize the kernel of \( Z(w) \), i.e. \( k : (w, w') \mapsto \mathrm{Cov}(Z(w), Z(w')) \). This kernel is a combination of a continuous part on \( \mathbb{R}^n \) and a discrete part \( Z_1 \times Z_2 \times \cdots \times Z_m \). Standard combinations are product and sum. In this post, I only consider the product: \[ k((x,z),(x',z')) = k_{\mathrm{cont}}(x,x') \cdot k_{\mathrm{cat}}(z,z') \] From now, suppose that \( m = 1 \) and we will only be interested in defining the kernel of the categorical variable \( k_{\mathrm{cat}}(\cdot,\cdot) \). Since the number of levels is finite, we can create a covariance matrix \( T \) satisfying \( T_{ij} = k_{\mathrm{cat}}(z_i, z_j') \), and this matrix is necessarily symmetric positive definite (SPD).

We are interested in finding the parametrization of the matrix \( T \) satisfying the SPD property. In this post, we pay attention to a class of hypersphere parametrizations: the Cholesky decomposition. A standard result is that for any SPD matrix, there exists one unique lower triangular matrix with positive diagonal. Besides, there are many other parametrizations; a summary can be found in [Perez et al., 2025].

Hypersphere Parametrizations as Riemannian Manifold

First, we rewrite \( T = LL^T \) where \( L \) is the lower triangular matrix. This matrix can be parametrized by the following heteroscedastic hypersphere (HE) decomposition: \begin{align*} &L_{1,1} = \theta_{1,0} \\ &L_{i,1} = \theta_{i,0}\cos(\theta_{i,1}), \quad \forall\, 2 \leq i \leq C \\ &L_{i,i} = \theta_{i,0}\prod_{l=1}^{i-1}\sin(\theta_{i,l}), \quad \forall\, 2 \leq i \leq C \\ &L_{i,j} = \theta_{i,0}\cos(\theta_{i,j})\prod_{l=1}^{j-1}\sin(\theta_{i,l}), \quad \forall\, 2 \leq j < i \leq C \end{align*} Furthermore, we assume that \( \theta_{i,j} \in (0,\pi) \) for \( 1 \leq j < i \leq C \) and \( \theta_{i,0}> 0 \) for \( 1 \leq i \leq C \). So the matrix \( L \) has positive diagonal and is described by the hyperparameter \( \theta = (\theta_{i,j})_{0 \leq j < i \leq C} \). We typically need \( \frac{C(C+1)}{2} \) parameters. One can then prove that there is a one-to-one correspondence between the set of SPD matrices and the set of all possible values of \( \theta \). Indeed, for every SPD matrix, there exists a unique lower triangular matrix with positive diagonal \( L \) satisfying the Cholesky decomposition, and \( L \) is uniquely determined by a hyperparameter \( \theta \).

From the hyperparameter estimation perspective, instead of estimating \( \theta \), we compute an estimate of the corresponding SPD matrix. We denote \( \mathrm{Sym}^+(n) \) the set of SPD matrices and \( \mathrm{GL}(n) \) the set of invertible matrices.

First, note that \( \mathrm{GL}(n) \) equipped with the Frobenius inner product forms a smooth Riemannian manifold. We want to prove that \( \mathrm{Sym}^+(n) \) is a smooth quotient manifold of \( \mathrm{GL}(n) \) through an equivalence relation. First, we need to define an equivalence relation on \( \mathrm{GL}(n) \) such that the quotient space \( \mathrm{GL}(n)/{\sim} \) is isomorphic to \( \mathrm{Sym}^+(n) \). Second, we need to define a natural projection from \( \mathrm{GL}(n) \) to \( \mathrm{Sym}^+(n) \) that is a smooth submersion. In fact, we proceed the second point first, defining the following projection: \[ \pi : A \in \mathrm{GL}(n) \mapsto \frac{1}{4}AA^T \in \mathrm{Sym}^+(n) \] \( \pi \) is a submersion iff at each \( A \in \mathrm{GL}(n) \) the differential map \( D_\pi(A) \) is surjective. We calculate its differential explicitly: \[ \pi(A + H) = \frac{1}{4}(A+H)(A+H)^T = \pi(A) + \frac{1}{4}(AH^T + HA^T) + \frac{HH^T}{4} \] So the differential map is: \[ D_\pi(A)H = \frac{1}{4}(AH^T + HA^T) \] Remark that the tangent space of \( \mathrm{Sym}^+(n) \) is exactly \( \mathrm{Sym}(n) \), the space of symmetric matrices. Indeed, \( \mathrm{Sym}^+(n) \) is an open subset of the vector space \( \mathrm{Sym}(n) \), so for each \( X \in \mathrm{Sym}^+(n) \), the tangent space \( T_X\mathrm{Sym}^+(n) = \mathrm{Sym}(n) \).

For the submersion part: for each \( X \in \mathrm{Sym}(n) \), let \( H = 2XA^{-T} \), then \( D_\pi(A)H = X \), where \( 2XA^{-T} \) can be understood as multiplying each element of \( X \) by 2. So \( \pi \) is a smooth submersion. By the projection, we define an equivalence relation \( \sim \) on \( \mathrm{GL}(n) \) by \( A \sim B \) iff \( AA^T = BB^T \). Then the quotient space \( \mathrm{GL}(n)/{\sim} \) is \( \{[A] \mid [A] = \{X \in \mathrm{GL}(n) : XX^T = AA^T\}\} \), and \( AA^T \) is exactly an element in \( \mathrm{Sym}^+(n) \), so there is a one-to-one correspondence between \( \mathrm{GL}(n)/{\sim} \) and \( \mathrm{Sym}^+(n) \), i.e. \( \mathrm{Sym}^+(n) \cong \mathrm{GL}(n)/{\sim} \). We can conclude that \( \mathrm{Sym}^+(n) \) is a quotient manifold of \( \mathrm{GL}(n) \).

Next, we need to define a Riemannian metric on \( \mathrm{Sym}^+(n) \) as well as the Riemannian distance with the corresponding Riemannian metric. In fact, there are several choices for the smooth Riemannian metric that lead to different distance formulas. Let us discuss some common choices (I will not go deeply into the construction or the intuition, but give a brief introduction about the definition and formula). The Riemannian metric is an inner product defined on the tangent space at each point of \( \mathrm{Sym}^+(n) \). Let \( X \) be a symmetric positive definite matrix; for \( U, V \in \mathrm{Sym}(n) \) seen as points on the tangent space at \( X \):

  • Affine invariant: \( \langle U, V\rangle_{X,\mathrm{AI}} = \mathrm{Tr}(X^{-1}UX^{-1}V) \)
  • Bures–Wasserstein: \( \langle U, V\rangle_{X,\mathrm{BW}} = \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(L_X(U)V) \), where \( L_X(U) \) is the solution to the matrix linear system \( L_X(U)X + XL_X(U) = U \). It has shown many benefits in Riemannian optimization for several problems; for a reference see [Han et al., 2021].
  • Log-Euclidean: \( \langle U, V\rangle_{X,\mathrm{LE}} = \langle D\mathrm{Log}_X(U), D\mathrm{Log}_X(V)\rangle_F \), where \( D\mathrm{Log}_X \) is the differential map at \( X \) and \( \mathrm{Log} \) is the function from \( \mathrm{Sym}^+(n) \) to \( \mathrm{Sym}(n) \), seen as the inverse of the \( \mathrm{Exp} \) function.
Summary. The HE parametrization for the kernel is a smooth manifold that can be equipped with several types of Riemannian metrics: Log-Euclidean, Affine Invariant, Bures–Wasserstein, etc.
Homoscedastic Hypersphere (Ho) Parametrization

If in the HE parametrization the diagonal of \( L \) is as we want, in this setting (Ho) we assign the diagonal of \( L \) to the vector \( \mathbf{1} \). This means that the number of parameters to be estimated reduces to \( \frac{C(C-1)}{2} \). Therefore, the kernel \( T \) is an SPD matrix with diagonal equal to 1. We denote \( \mathrm{Cor}^+(n) \) the set of correlation positive definite matrices, which is a subset of \( \mathrm{Sym}^+(n) \) with \( \mathrm{diag}(X) = \mathbf{1} \) for \( X \in \mathrm{Cor}^+(n) \). We can easily prove that there is a one-to-one correspondence between the set of all possible \( \theta \) and \( \mathrm{Cor}^+(n) \). In this section, we try to specify a Riemannian structure for \( \mathrm{Cor}^+(n) \). An excellent reference is [David & Gu, 2019]. We restate the core theorem of their work here:

Theorem
The Lie group \( \mathrm{Diag}^+(n) \) of diagonal matrices with positive entries acts smoothly, properly, and freely on \( \mathrm{Sym}^+(n) \) as a map \( \mathrm{Diag}^+(n) \times \mathrm{Sym}^+(n) \to \mathrm{Sym}^+(n) \) given by \( (D, P) \mapsto DPD \). Subsequently, the quotient manifold \( \mathrm{Sym}^+(n)/\mathrm{Diag}^+(n) \) resulting from this group action is a smooth manifold in which every element can be uniquely expressed by an element of \( \mathrm{Cor}^+(n) \).

The full proof can be found in [David & Gu, 2019]. Now, to define the natural projection (which is not specified in their work), we trace back to the group action \( A \mapsto DAD \), so for each \( A \in \mathrm{Sym}^+(n) \), the orbit is \( [A] = \{DAD \mid D \in \mathrm{Diag}^+(n)\} \). We need to define a bijection from \( \mathrm{Sym}^+(n)/\mathrm{Diag}^+(n) \) to \( \mathrm{Cor}^+(n) \). In fact, we can define the following symmetric scaling: for each \( [A] \in \mathrm{Sym}^+(n)/\mathrm{Diag}^+(n) \): \[ p([A]) = \mathrm{Diag}(A)^{-1/2}\, A\, \mathrm{Diag}(A)^{-1/2} \] Actually, the bijectivity of this map can be proved as follows. This map is injective: if there exist \( A, B \in \mathrm{Sym}^+(n) \) such that \( \mathrm{Diag}(A)^{-1/2} A \mathrm{Diag}(A)^{-1/2} = \mathrm{Diag}(B)^{-1/2} B \mathrm{Diag}(B)^{-1/2} \), by multiplying matrices on both sides and noting that diagonal matrices commute, we can easily see that \( B \in [A] \), i.e. \( [A] = [B] \). Note that if \( B = p(A) \), then \( B_{ii} = 1 \) and \( B_{ij} = \frac{A_{ij}}{\sqrt{A_{ii}}\sqrt{A_{jj}}} \). For surjectivity, let \( B \in \mathrm{Cor}^+(n) \) and for all \( i \), \( a_{ii} > 0 \); then we can construct a matrix \( A \) satisfying: \[ A_{ii} = a_{ii}, \quad A_{ij} = B_{ij}\sqrt{a_{ii} a_{jj}} \] To prove that \( A \in \mathrm{Sym}^+(n) \), we rely on the definition of symmetric positive definite. We have \( B = \mathrm{Diag}(A)^{-1/2} A \mathrm{Diag}(A)^{-1/2} \) and \( \langle Bx, x\rangle > 0 \) for all \( x \neq 0 \), then: \[ \langle \mathrm{Diag}(A)^{-1/2} A \mathrm{Diag}(A)^{-1/2} x, x\rangle = \langle A\, \mathrm{Diag}(A)^{-1/2} x,\, \mathrm{Diag}(A)^{-1/2} x\rangle = \langle By, y\rangle > 0 \] The above inequality uses the symmetry of diagonal matrices and the positivity of diagonal entries (which implies \( y \neq 0 \)). To conclude, this map is bijective, so a natural projection is defined as: \[ \pi(A) = \mathrm{Diag}(A)^{-1/2}\, A\, \mathrm{Diag}(A)^{-1/2} \] Now, to define the Riemannian metric as well as the corresponding distance for numerical implementations: most works on Riemannian metrics are only useful in theory and seem inapplicable to implement in practice. A potential choice is the framework developed also in [David & Gu, 2019]. A weak point in their work may be the lack of a closed form for the distance and geodesics. Therefore, they overcame this point by providing an approximate alternative.

Conclusion

Besides the 2 parametrizations of the kernel discussed above, we may consider a low-rank version, i.e. \( T = LL^T \) where \( L \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times q} \), \( q < n \), of rank \( q \). This could lead to a reduction in computational complexity, but I will reserve it for the future.

Together, we studied that 2 parametrizations of the kernel admit a Riemannian manifold structure. In terms of GP with categorical inputs, the problem of estimating kernels could become an optimization problem over a matrix manifold — i.e. symmetric positive definite matrices — which has been an active research field in recent years.
